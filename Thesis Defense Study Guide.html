1 The central argument

Your strongest defensible conclusion: In this healthy juvenile female porcine ESHP model, low glucose and insulin provision reduced heart weight gain and selected tissue injury signals, but did not sustain function as well as high glucose and insulin. Adding β-hydroxybutyrate to the low glucose and insulin condition maintained cardiac index and stroke work relative to low glucose and insulin alone, while retaining lower edema, MDA, and TNF-α than high glucose and insulin. The study supports further development of the combined strategy; it does not establish its mechanism, an optimal dose, non-inferiority to high glucose and insulin, or improved transplant outcomes.

A 30 second answer. “I investigated whether changing metabolic substrate provision improves donor heart preservation during normothermic ex situ perfusion. We compared high glucose and insulin, low glucose and insulin, and low glucose and insulin with β-hydroxybutyrate, with six hearts assigned per group. Low glucose and insulin reduced edema and selected injury markers but allowed function to decline. Ketone supplementation preserved function while retaining the favorable tissue profile. The main next steps are to control sodium and osmolality, measure substrate oxidation directly, and test recipient outcomes.”

A two minute answer. “The clinical problem is that donor hearts are scarce and their preservation time is constrained. Ex situ heart perfusion extends the preservation and assessment window, but hearts can still deteriorate on the circuit. Previous work from our group implicates oxidative stress and metabolic changes, including altered glucose handling. I therefore tested a combined reduction in glucose and insulin, with or without β-hydroxybutyrate. In blood and modified Krebs–Henseleit perfusate at 38.5°C, the three conditions separated function from selected tissue injury outcomes. At T7, cardiac index was approximately 89% of baseline with ketones, 87% with high glucose and insulin, and 63% with low glucose and insulin. Reported weight gain was approximately 10%, 25%, and 6%, respectively. MDA and TNF-α were lower in both low glucose and insulin conditions than in high glucose and insulin. The ketone group nevertheless developed higher sodium and lactate. Thus, this is evidence for a useful preservation phenotype under the combined intervention, with important formulation and mechanistic questions remaining. The biochemical data suggest hypotheses; they do not demonstrate ketone oxidation, improved ATP efficiency, or increased mitophagic flux. A sodium and osmolality controlled study with direct flux measurements, followed by transplantation, would be the logical progression.”

2 Detailed thesis summary

2.1 The clinical problem and why preservation matters

Chapter 1 begins with heart failure and progresses through drug treatment, mechanical support, transplantation, and donor preservation. Its purpose is to explain why improving preservation could benefit patients even without changing donor availability: a longer safe preservation window can expand geographic sharing, allow assessment of uncertain hearts, and create time for treatment. Better preservation might also support use of marginal donors.

The thesis quotes approximately 750,000 Canadians living with HF, more than 100,000 annual diagnoses, 71,000 hospitalizations, and more than 6,500 deaths. These are background estimates from its cited source, not results of this study. Memorize their jurisdiction, year, denominator, and source if using them. The distinction between cardiovascular disease and heart failure matters: the broad claim that cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of death should not be converted into “heart failure is the leading cause of all global mortality.”

The literature review distinguishes HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF and describes NYHA classes and AHA stages. Know that NYHA class describes functional limitation, while AHA stage describes progression from risk to advanced disease. Some threshold wording and treatment descriptions need correction, listed in Part 5. The review's role in the defense is to establish the transplantation problem; it is not necessary to defend every historical device statistic as current.

Mechanical support offers a bridge to transplantation, a bridge to candidacy, or destination therapy. An LVAD augments systemic circulation; an RVAD supports pulmonary circulation; a total artificial heart replaces the ventricles. Do not generalize every VAD as pulsatile: device physiology varies. Transplantation remains the definitive replacement option for selected patients with advanced disease, but donor supply, ischemic time, donor injury, and recipient factors constrain its use.

2.2 Donor type and ischemia reperfusion injury

DBD and DCD hearts enter preservation with different physiological histories. Brain death can produce neurohormonal and inflammatory injury even though circulation is maintained. DCD hearts experience an agonal period and warm ischemia around withdrawal of support and circulatory arrest. Your experimental hearts were healthy anesthetized donor animals undergoing controlled procurement; they did not reproduce either a full brain death syndrome or a clinically representative DCD warm ischemic insult.

The thesis outlines ATP depletion, failure of ion homeostasis, intracellular sodium and calcium accumulation, and injury on reperfusion. A useful causal explanation is: inadequate oxygen limits oxidative phosphorylation; ATP dependent transport becomes impaired; acidosis and sodium loading alter exchangers; calcium loading, rapid pH restoration, and ROS promote mitochondrial permeability transition during reperfusion. Avoid saying that lactate formation itself releases the proton causing acidosis: the LDH reaction consumes a proton, while the overall ischemic metabolic state and ATP turnover contribute to acid accumulation.

This matters because an intervention useful in healthy perfused hearts may behave differently in a heart with impaired mitochondrial oxidative capacity. Ketones bypass parts of glucose metabolism, but still require functioning mitochondrial machinery and oxygen for sustained oxidative ATP generation.

2.3 Preservation technologies

Static cold storage is simple and slows metabolism, but provides no continuous oxygenated circulation, limited assessment, and no comparable treatment platform. A practical traditional cold ischemic window is often discussed around four to six hours; this is a risk continuum, not a universal time at which every heart becomes nonviable.

The thesis reviews preservation solutions and additives. Intracellular type solutions generally emphasize high potassium and low sodium; extracellular type solutions use a different electrolyte balance. Buffering, osmotic agents, antioxidants, arrest, and calcium control each address a different component of preservation injury. The thesis discusses HTK, modified HTK, Celsior, del Nido, Plegisol, and University of Wisconsin solution, as well as HEMO₂life, melatonin, and adenosine–lidocaine based formulations. These illustrate that composition can influence recovery independently of the machine.

Hypothermic machine perfusion maintains cold circulation and oxygen delivery with low metabolic demand. It can improve preservation without requiring a working heart, but an arrested heart cannot provide direct contractile performance measurements during preservation. Normothermic perfusion supports a beating heart and more active metabolism. Some experimental platforms permit working mode assessment, whereas a beating unloaded clinical platform does not automatically measure useful external cardiac work. Distinguish temperature, ventricular loading, and oxygen delivery when comparing systems.

The clinical literature provides several kinds of evidence: randomized comparisons such as PROCEED II, trials of hypothermic oxygenated perfusion, studies of DCD transplantation, and observational device registries. Trial design matters. A non-inferiority result answers a different question from superiority; a favorable secondary endpoint cannot rescue an unsuccessful primary superiority endpoint. See the paper guide for exact cited trials and their limitations.

2.4 Why metabolism became the experimental target

The mechanistic premise is that the isolated perfusion circuit lacks whole body homeostasis. It does not continuously replace or clear substrates in the same manner as the liver, kidney, endocrine system, lymphatic circulation, and systemic circulation. The blood component also remains metabolically active and can be injured by recirculation.

The thesis identifies three interacting contributors to oxidative stress: hemolysis, inadequate antioxidant compensation, and metabolic dysregulation. Cell free hemoglobin and heme can contribute to vascular and oxidative injury; antioxidant systems may become depleted; mitochondrial electron handling and glucose related pathways may change. Hemopexin binds heme and haptoglobin binds free hemoglobin. They are binding proteins, not simply antioxidant enzymes. The circuit also contains leukocytes and platelets, so tissue and perfusate signals need not originate solely from cardiomyocytes.

Previous ESHP work cited in the thesis reported functional decline, oxidative injury, altered pyruvate kinase activity, and alterations involving the PPP and HBP. These observations motivate the intervention. They do not prove that every heart in this experiment had a pyruvate kinase bottleneck or that it was the cause of functional decline. This study did not directly measure glycolytic flux or identify a rate limiting reaction by isotope tracing.

Reducing glucose and insulin was intended to decrease excessive nutrient signaling and glucose diversion into pathways associated with injury. However, glucose concentration below 2 mmol/L should be described precisely as the experimental low glucose target, rather than equated with ordinary physiological human fasting glycemia. Restricting glucose in an isolated circuit is also different from whole body fasting, which supplies fatty acids, ketones, and hormonal adaptations.

2.5 Why β hydroxybutyrate was selected

The heart can oxidize multiple fuels. BHB can enter mitochondrial ketolysis and yield acetyl-CoA without requiring glycolysis or PDH conversion of pyruvate. This gives a plausible route around altered glucose processing. The heart failure literature supports increased ketone use in some disease settings and shows that ketone utilization can be adaptive, but failing hearts and healthy donor hearts on ESHP are different systems.

BHB is also associated with signaling effects in other models, including effects on inflammation, antioxidant responses, and protein modifications. Those effects are candidates to test here. The present experiment does not separate oxidation from signaling, sodium loading, osmolality, substrate competition, or changes in blood cell metabolism.

Importantly, the low GI groups were not free of other fuels. Modified Krebs–Henseleit buffer contained sodium pyruvate and sodium acetate, and whole blood carried endogenous substrates. This makes “ketones rescued an otherwise fuel free heart” inaccurate. The defensible observation is that adding the BHB formulation improved preservation relative to the otherwise matched low GI condition.

2.6 Hypothesis and design

The thesis hypothesized that low glucose and insulin, with or without ketones, would improve metabolic efficiency, reduce ROS, and preserve function. This is a multipart hypothesis. The low GI group showed favorable selected injury measures but poorer function. Therefore, the full hypothesis was only partially supported. The ketone supplemented strategy produced the more favorable combination of outcomes.

Eighteen hearts were assigned across three groups of six: high GI, low GI, and low GI plus BHB. Glucose and insulin changed together, so the high versus low GI comparison estimates the effect of a combined intervention. It cannot assign the benefit or harm separately to glucose and insulin. There was no high GI plus BHB arm, so an interaction between baseline GI and BHB cannot be fully estimated.

The model used juvenile female Yorkshire pigs, approximately 45–55 kg and 16–18 weeks old. The perfusate began with approximately 800 mL donor whole blood and 800 mL modified Krebs–Henseleit buffer. The circuit used two centrifugal pumps, an oxygenator, heat exchanger, arterial filter, infusion pumps, and flow measurements. Working mode LA filling pressure was 6 mmHg and temperature 38.5°C. Dobutamine, heparin, and antibiotics were additional exposures.

There is an unresolved timing discrepancy: the abstract describes seven hours, methods describe eight, and captions describe six hours of working mode. Plots show T1, T3, T5, and T7, with T1 normalized to 100%. A possible explanation is separate total and working mode intervals, but it is not established by this version. Verify the experimental timeline before presenting a definitive duration.

2.7 What was measured

Functional measurements assessed preservation of cardiac performance relative to baseline: stroke work, cardiac index, maximum and minimum dP/dt, flow, systolic pressure, and oxygen consumption. Cardiac index here means LA flow divided by pre-perfusion heart mass, with units mL/min/g. This is different from clinical cardiac index normalized to body surface area.

Perfusate measurements tracked electrolytes and lactate, while group targets controlled glucose, insulin provision, and BHB concentration. The document lists blood gas and metabolic monitoring, but does not provide enough dosing detail to reconstruct total BHB administration. An achieved concentration is different from an infusion rate or total dose.

Heart weight change was used as an edema surrogate. Tissue analysis principally used terminal LV homogenates and Western blots. These are mixed tissue samples unless cardiomyocyte isolation was separately performed; “LV myocytes” is too specific for a homogenization procedure alone. Immunoblot signals report relative abundance or phosphorylation ratios, rather than absolute metabolite concentrations. MDA and 4-HNE immunoreactivity reflect lipid peroxidation related adducts, not direct instantaneous ROS generation.

2.8 The functional result

At T3 the major performance endpoints did not separate clearly. By T5, stroke work and cardiac index were lower in low GI than in both high GI and ketone supplemented low GI. At T7 the differences were substantial. Cardiac index retained approximately 89% of baseline with ketones and 87% with high GI, versus 63% with low GI. Stroke work retained approximately 88%, 75%, and 53%, respectively.

No significant ketone versus high GI difference was detected in cardiac index or stroke work. This supports “no difference detected” in this experiment; it does not show equivalence or non-inferiority. The study was small and did not use a specified equivalence margin.

Maximum dP/dt at T7 was significantly better preserved with ketones than low GI; high GI versus low GI was reported as p=0.0643. Systolic pressure was better maintained with ketones and high GI than low GI. The minimum dP/dt narrative contradicts itself: its final comparison and Figure 6D report high GI better than low GI at T7, despite an earlier sentence claiming no differences at any time. Coronary flow showed no significant group differences. The text calls this pulmonary artery flow, while Figure 6E calls it coronary flow; on this circuit the PA can collect coronary venous drainage, but this is not evidence of full RV output preservation.

Oxygen consumption was higher in high GI than low GI at T3 and T5, with no significant group difference at T7. Lower oxygen consumption alone does not establish better efficiency: it can also accompany less cardiac work. A valid efficiency argument requires simultaneous work and oxygen consumption under comparable loading, plus an appropriate analysis of their relationship.

2.9 Electrolytes, lactate, and weight gain

The low GI group developed the highest potassium, reaching 6.57 mmol/L at T7, versus 5.00 with ketones and 5.37 with high GI. Potential contributors include insulin dependent cellular potassium uptake, ATP dependent transport, hemolysis, and tissue injury. The data do not isolate which dominates. Importantly, low GI and ketone groups had the same insulin infusion rate, so their potassium difference cannot be attributed solely to a difference in prescribed insulin.

The ketone group developed increasing sodium, reaching 169.2 mmol/L at T7, versus 151.5 in low GI and 147.8 in high GI. Sodium BHB introduced sodium alongside the substrate. Hypertonicity may also affect water distribution, coronary physiology, blood cells, and the edema measurement. Thus, reduced weight gain cannot be attributed exclusively to lower inflammation or ROS without sodium and osmolality controls.

Lactate was highest with ketones at T7: 6.10 mmol/L, versus 4.28 in high GI and 2.15 in low GI. This is an important dissociation: the group with the best retained performance did not have the lowest lactate. Ketone competition with glucose oxidation is one possible explanation, but blood cell glycolysis, altered uptake, redox state, inadequate regional oxygenation, and lack of systemic clearance also matter. Concentration alone does not identify the source or flux of lactate. It cannot yet be used as a validated ketone dosing target.

Reported heart weight gain was 25.16% in high GI, 10.11% with ketones, and 5.85% in low GI. Both low GI strategies differed significantly from high GI, with no significant ketone versus low GI difference. However, Figure 4 and the results text disagree on the edema denominator, so the reported percentages must be traced to the calculation file before being described definitively as percent of initial weight.

2.10 Tissue results and their boundaries

The most coherent tissue findings were lower MDA and TNF-α in both low GI conditions than high GI. Cysteine sulfonation did not significantly differ. The 4-HNE graph supports high GI greater than ketones, but the prose assigns the smaller printed mean to high GI and larger mean to ketones. The likely swapped numeric labels require confirmation against raw data.

The broad ER stress response did not uniformly change. PDI was higher in high GI than in vivo tissue; ATF6, ERO-1, IRE1 related measures, and CHOP did not show significant differences. It is therefore too strong to say that the intervention definitively suppressed ER stress as a pathway. PDI can participate in adaptive protein folding, and total abundance does not establish enzyme activity or ROS generation.

AKT Thr308 phosphorylation relative to total AKT was higher in high GI than both low GI conditions and in vivo. This is consistent with the different insulin exposure. Ser473 AKT and AMPK findings depend on whether the in vivo group is included: the full comparison was nonsignificant, while selected perfused-group analyses identified differences. Treat these as analysis-sensitive findings requiring a prespecified rationale and transparent reporting.

Global O-GlcNAcylation was elevated in ketone and high GI tissue relative to low GI and in vivo. This is a major challenge to a simple story that “more O-GlcNAc always causes more injury”: ketone tissue retained function and had lower MDA and TNF-α despite elevated O-GlcNAc. Acute versus chronic exposure, protein targets, cellular compartment, and OGT/OGA activity matter. The study did not measure HBP flux directly.

Complex I and III immunoblot abundance was lower in high GI than in vivo. Complex II and V showed no significant differences. These are abundance findings, not measurements of electron transport activity, respiration, ATP synthesis, or mitochondrial number. A difference from in vivo in one group, coupled with a nonsignificant difference in another, is not itself proof of a significant treatment versus treatment difference.

2.11 Discussion, limitations, and next experiments

The discussion proposes nutrient sensing, improved mitochondrial quality, AMPK signaling, sirtuins, autophagy, and ketone supported energy supply as explanations. They form a useful experimental agenda. They are not measured mediators in this dataset: there were no direct ATP flux, ketone tracer, NAD redox, sirtuin activity, or autophagic flux assays.

The largest translational limitation is the lack of recipient transplantation. On-circuit function does not establish tolerance of implantation, second ischemia/reperfusion, bypass separation, RV loading, rejection, PGD, or recipient survival. Healthy juvenile female hearts also do not capture older, comorbid, brain dead, or DCD donors. Terminal LV tissue cannot describe temporal changes or the entire heart.

The thesis describes practical attempts to supply fatty acids, including sodium palmitate and Intralipid. These are informative formulation problems rather than completed comparative efficacy experiments. Palmitate solubility, albumin binding, the unbound FFA fraction, triglyceride hydrolysis, heparin effects on lipoprotein lipase, and species specific reactions all need control. Avoid claiming that failing hearts universally increase fatty acid oxidation; metabolic remodeling depends on stage, etiology, and context.

A strong next study would compare low GI plus BHB with low GI plus sodium/osmolality matched control, define a reproducible BHB formulation and dose, quantify substrate oxidation and energetic state, and then test transplantation under clinically relevant donor injury conditions. The eventual goal is a preservation strategy that improves usable graft and recipient outcomes, with feasible electrolyte, acid base, blood compatibility, and logistical control.

4 Pathways and concepts you should be able to explain aloud

4.1 Ketolysis and its stoichiometry

The essential sequence is BHB → acetoacetate → acetoacetyl-CoA → two acetyl-CoA → TCA cycle → reducing equivalents → ETC and ATP synthesis.

BDH1 catalyzes BHB + NAD⁺ ⇌ acetoacetate + NADH + H⁺. SCOT, encoded by OXCT1, transfers CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate, producing acetoacetyl-CoA and succinate. Mitochondrial thiolase uses another CoA to cleave acetoacetyl-CoA into two acetyl-CoA. This is thiolysis, not simple hydrolysis, and it is ketolysis rather than fatty acid β-oxidation. BHB oxidation generates NADH at its first step. The net redox effect in a cell cannot be inferred solely from the supplied substrate. Primary BDH1 study.

For a deeper energetic question, an optional theoretical calculation assumes 2.5 ATP per NADH, 1.5 per FADH₂, and 10 ATP per acetyl-CoA oxidized in the TCA cycle. Accounting for the succinyl-CoA synthetase opportunity cost of SCOT gives acetoacetate ≈2×10−1=19 ATP and D-BHB ≈19+2.5=21.5 ATP. These are calculated approximations under those assumptions, not measurements from your thesis or proof of better mechanical efficiency. BHB supplies acetyl units but is not net anaplerosis; TCA intermediate replenishment can still matter. You do not need to lead with theoretical ATP yields unless asked.

The liver is principally a ketone producer and lacks canonical SCOT-dependent terminal ketolysis in hepatocytes. Do not say it relies heavily on ketones as its fasting fuel. Red cells lack mitochondria and cannot oxidize BHB through this pathway; they depend on glycolysis and use the PPP for redox defense. This is central to interpreting a blood based circuit.

4.2 Glucose metabolism and competing pathways

Glucose enters cells and is phosphorylated to glucose-6-phosphate. Glycolysis supplies pyruvate and substrate-level ATP. Pyruvate can form lactate through LDH or enter mitochondria and be converted by PDH into acetyl-CoA. Ketolysis bypasses glycolysis and PDH; it does not bypass a requirement for mitochondrial oxidation.

The oxidative PPP starts from glucose-6-phosphate through G6PD and generates NADPH. NADPH supports glutathione reductase, which regenerates GSH from GSSG. NADPH also supplies NOX enzymes. Therefore, NADPH and PPP activity are not inherently harmful: interpretation requires flux, redox demand, and the downstream enzyme consuming reducing power. In red cells, reduced glucose availability can compromise ATP and antioxidant support even if the myocardium has an alternative mitochondrial fuel.

The HBP branches from fructose-6-phosphate through GFAT, integrating glutamine and other metabolic inputs to generate UDP-GlcNAc. OGT adds O-GlcNAc to protein serine/threonine residues and OGA removes it. O-GlcNAcylation is enzymatic and reversible; it is not the same as nonenzymatic glycation or conventional secretory N-linked glycosylation. Its effects depend on the modified protein, site, timing, and context.

Your data show elevated O-GlcNAc in both high GI and ketones despite different injury profiles. An experiment using OGT/OGA perturbation and specific target readouts would be needed to determine whether the modification mediates benefit or injury. Acute increases have been cardioprotective in perfused rat heart I/R experiments, making blanket claims of toxicity inappropriate. Primary OGA inhibition study.

4.3 Insulin AKT AMPK mTOR and autophagy

Insulin signaling through its receptor, IRS, PI3K, and PIP₃ recruits downstream signaling including AKT. PDK1 is associated with AKT Thr308 phosphorylation and mTORC2 with Ser473. These sites are related but not interchangeable. AKT influences glucose transport, survival, growth, and nutrient signaling; lower AKT phosphorylation cannot automatically be labeled better health. Prescribed insulin delivery is not the same as measured circulating insulin concentration: endogenous insulin, adsorption and degradation can alter exposure.

AMPK senses energetic stress through AMP/ADP related regulation and activating phosphorylation, commonly discussed at Thr172. It can favor energy production and suppress energy consuming pathways. AMPK–ULK1 signaling can promote autophagy initiation; mTORC1 can inhibit this process under nutrient abundance. A single p-mTOR/total result does not represent every mTOR complex or downstream output.

What you actually measured: terminal phosphorylation ratios. What remains to test: energetic charge, kinase activity, downstream substrates, autophagic turnover, and selective mitochondrial clearance. Static markers and pathway phosphorylation cannot establish autophagic flux. Use time-resolved LC3 turnover with suitable lysosomal controls, cargo turnover, or validated mitochondrial delivery reporters in an appropriate model. Autophagy assay guidance.

4.4 Mitochondria oxidative injury and sirtuins

Complex I accepts electrons from NADH; complex II links succinate oxidation with the respiratory chain; complex III transfers electrons onward through cytochrome c; complex IV reduces oxygen to water; complex V uses the proton gradient for ATP synthesis. Complex II does not pump protons. Electron leak can generate ROS, but abundance alone cannot identify respiratory competence or electron leak rate.

MDA and 4-HNE relate to lipid peroxidation. Cysteine sulfonation reflects an oxidative protein modification. ROMO1 is a protein associated with mitochondrial redox regulation. None of these substitutes for direct mitochondrial ROS production measured under defined respiratory conditions. A healthy response can include changes in both production and scavenging, and apparent ROS signals can depend on assay chemistry.

Sirtuins are NAD⁺ dependent deacylases with distinct compartments and substrates; SIRT1 is predominantly discussed in nuclear/cytosolic regulation and SIRT3 in mitochondrial protein regulation. The thesis suggests SIRT–PGC-1α–antioxidant/mitochondrial quality pathways. No NAD pool, sirtuin activity, protein acetylation, or PGC-1α result was measured here. Present this as a mechanistic proposal rather than an established sequence caused by BHB.

Antioxidant Nrf2 is encoded by NFE2L2. KEAP1 normally promotes its degradation; stabilization allows nuclear accumulation and binding to antioxidant response elements, with increased expression of genes such as NQO1 and HMOX1. These response elements are DNA sequences, not antioxidant enzymes. Distinguish this Nrf2 from nuclear respiratory factor 2/GABP in mitochondrial biogenesis literature. Your experiment did not establish Nrf2 activation or dependence; the cited Kovac paper in Part 9 is useful for understanding why its effects on net ROS can be complex.

4.5 ER stress inflammation and endothelial permeability

The unfolded protein response includes IRE1, PERK, and ATF6 arms. Activated IRE1 can splice XBP1; ATF6 activation involves cleavage and translocation; PERK can signal through eIF2α, ATF4, and CHOP. Your assay panel sampled some of these components but did not comprehensively demonstrate pathway activation. PDI assists disulfide bond formation/rearrangement; ERO1 helps reoxidize PDI and can generate peroxide. More PDI is not inherently proof of pathological ER stress.

NF-κB activation can involve IκB regulation and nuclear translocation, so unchanged total abundance is not equivalent to unchanged transcriptional activity. TNF-α can affect endothelial signaling and permeability, but lower tissue TNF-α does not prove it caused the reduced weight gain. BHB associated NLRP3 effects in other models are interesting background; this study did not measure NLRP3, caspase-1, mature IL-1β, or IL-18 to establish inflammasome inhibition.

4.6 Edema and integrated circulation

Fluid movement depends on hydrostatic pressure, oncotic forces, endothelial permeability, interstitial conditions, and lymphatic clearance. In your apparatus, albumin concentration, dilution, perfusion pressure, sodium/osmolality, vascular damage, and absent normal lymphatic drainage can all influence heart weight. Thus, “less weight gain” is a useful phenotype but not a specific assay of endothelial protection.

The ketone group introduces a particularly important competing explanation: increased sodium and tonicity may reduce tissue water while independently changing blood cell and myocardial physiology. A matched sodium/osmolality control and direct wet/dry ratio or tissue water measurement would help distinguish reduced water content from reduced injury. Low GI also had reduced weight gain without the same sodium elevation, so sodium cannot explain the entire three-group pattern.

5.2 Mechanistic statements to correct or qualify

Ketones are not metabolized by fatty acid β-oxidation. Use BDH1 → SCOT/OXCT1 → thiolase. The initial BHB oxidation produces NADH. Do not claim an elevated NAD⁺/NADH ratio or activated sirtuins in your samples without measurement.

The liver does not ordinarily rely on canonical ketone oxidation as its fasting fuel. Its principal role is ketogenesis; lack of hepatic SCOT prevents normal terminal ketolysis. This changes the interpretation of proposed extension to liver perfusion.

Low GI is not “fasting” in the full physiological sense. Glucose <2 mmol/L is a restriction condition, and the circuit lacks the whole body adaptations and fuel delivery of fasting. Both low GI groups still receive other substrates from the buffer and blood.

AMPK and autophagy claims exceed the results. The full AMPK comparison was null, and no autophagic flux assay was performed. No significant p-mTOR difference does not establish an mTOR-independent mechanism. Similarly, ETC abundance does not establish preserved mitochondrial activity.

Lactate does not prove sufficient acetyl-CoA supply or efficient ketone oxidation. Its concentration is a net circuit balance. It may become a useful biomarker only after validation against dose, flux, tissue viability, and recipient outcomes.

O-GlcNAc is not uniformly harmful. Your own ketone result argues against a simple one-direction model. Acute cardioprotection has been demonstrated in other perfused-heart settings, while chronic excess can have different effects.

Hypernatremia cannot be declared safe from a single-cell in silico study. It does not test whole-heart perfusion, red cells, endothelial permeability, or transplantation. Sodium and osmolality are mechanistic confounders as well as formulation constraints. Cited modeling study.

6 How to defend the design and statistics

Your experimental unit is the donor heart, not a time point, tissue aliquot, lane, or band. Repeated measurements within a heart are correlated. A group-by-time model should account for that correlation, with repeated-measures ANOVA or a suitable mixed model depending on assumptions and missingness. The thesis's phrase “two-way ANOVA” does not establish that this was done. Retrieve the Prism setup rather than guessing.

Tukey correction addresses a defined set of pairwise comparisons; it does not automatically correct every test across dozens of biomarkers and time points. The method description says it was applied to account for unequal group sizes, but its principal purpose is multiple pairwise comparison control; the appropriate unequal-n implementation and variance assumptions should be checked separately. Identify a primary outcome and label broad biochemical work as exploratory where appropriate.

With n=6 per group, a nonsignificant result is compatible with a range of effects. Do not say that ketones and high GI were equivalent. Report effect estimates and confidence intervals if the raw analysis can provide them, and distinguish lack of evidence from an adequately powered equivalence test. Likewise, p=.0643 is not statistical significance and should not become an affirmative claim of benefit.

Baseline normalization helps compare relative preservation but can conceal absolute differences and introduce denominator noise. Be ready to show absolute baseline and endpoint values, donor heart weights, support requirements, and group balance. The CI formula uses pre-perfusion mass, so terminal edema should not directly alter its denominator if implemented as written.

Removing a variable in vivo group changes the residual variance and family of comparisons. A perfused-only analysis may be scientifically sensible if specified in advance; excluding a group because a preferred p-value appears is not. Report the full and restricted models transparently and explain why each addresses a distinct question. “Significant versus control” in one group and “nonsignificant versus control” in another does not establish a direct difference between those groups.

For Western blots, know biological n, technical replicates, lane order, inter-gel standards, normalization, saturation checks, band selection, antibody specificity, and whether analysis was blinded. Global adduct signals can depend on molecular weight ranges and integration choices. Missing antioxidant, respiration, and flux assays limit mechanistic conclusions even when a band difference is clear.

A strong response to a limitation: state the observation, acknowledge the alternative explanation, then name the decisive experiment. For example: “The ketone group had less weight gain and lower MDA, but sodium also rose. We therefore cannot assign the water effect entirely to reduced oxidative injury. A sodium and osmolality matched control with tissue water and endothelial permeability measurements would distinguish those explanations.”

7 Experiments worth proposing

  1. Formulation and osmotic controls. Low GI vehicle versus low GI BHB with sodium/osmolality matching, controlled pH and volume, and a documented D-BHB or racemic formulation. Measure achieved concentrations, sodium, measured osmolality, acid base balance, tissue water, and blood compatibility.
  2. Direct metabolic tracing. Labeled BHB and glucose with appropriate arterial/venous sampling, isotopomer modeling, oxygen measurements, and energetic assays. Distinguish uptake from oxidation and from TCA pool labeling; examine pyruvate/acetate contributions already in the buffer.
  3. Separate glucose from insulin. A factorial design can estimate their independent effects and interaction. Add a high GI plus BHB arm if asking whether BHB benefit depends on the background substrate strategy.
  4. Blood component mechanism. Quantify plasma free Hb, heme, haptoglobin/hemopexin, hemolysis, RBC ATP/redox state, hematocrit, and oxygen delivery. Compare with a validated alternative perfusate only if this preserves meaningful physiological comparability.
  5. Mitochondrial and quality control mechanism. Respirometry, ATP/ADP/AMP and NAD compartment-aware measurements, ETC activity/assembly, direct ROS assays, and dynamic autophagy/mitophagy assays. Use perturbations only with awareness of off-target and systemic effects.
  6. Clinically relevant transplantation. Standardized DCD or brain death injury, controlled recipient conditions, and endpoints including bypass separation, biventricular function, PGD-like support needs, injury, and survival. Predefine allocation, blinding, primary outcome, exclusion rules, and a power calculation using a clinically meaningful effect.

1 Hatami and colleagues 2019 on myocardial functional decline

Thesis references 96 and 109. Myocardial Functional Decline During Prolonged Ex Situ Heart Perfusion. Annals of Thoracic Surgery. DOI 10.1016/j.athoracsur.2019.01.076. Verification: primary abstract.

Healthy porcine hearts underwent 12 hours of working or nonworking ESHP. At hour 11, cardiac index relative to hour 1 was 33% in working mode and 10% in nonworking mode, p=.025. 2 Hatami and colleagues 2023 on oxidative stress during ESHP

Twenty difficult practice questions

1. Walk through BHB oxidation, with enzymes, compartments, carbon fate and redox cofactors. Then correct the statement in your discussion that ketones undergo beta-oxidation and generate NAD+.

Answer direction: In mitochondria, BDH1 oxidizes D-BHB to acetoacetate while reducing NAD+ to NADH. SCOT/OXCT1 transfers CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate, producing acetoacetyl-CoA and succinate; thiolase cleaves acetoacetyl-CoA with CoA into two acetyl-CoA. This is ketolysis, distinct from fatty-acid beta-oxidation, and it does not establish a rise in NAD+/NADH. Thesis: §§1.17 and 4.5; Fig 2. Relevant primary work: Aubert et al 2016.

2. If BHB bypasses a pyruvate-kinase bottleneck, what metabolic defects does it fail to bypass?

Answer direction: It can deliver acetyl-CoA without passing through glycolysis and PDH, but still needs transport, ketolytic enzymes, CoA availability, adequate TCA-cycle function, oxygen and an effective ETC. It cannot rescue every mitochondrial lesion, and acetyl-CoA provision does not by itself provide net TCA anaplerosis in mammalian myocardium. Distinguish these biochemical requirements from mechanisms measured in your study. Thesis: §§1.15.1, 1.17 and 4.5.

3. What experiment would demonstrate that BHB was actually oxidized by your hearts rather than merely present in the reservoir?

Answer direction: Combine arterial-venous BHB concentration differences and coronary flow with labeled-BHB tracing into acetyl-CoA/TCA intermediates and, where feasible, CO2. Correct for circuit volume, infusion, sampling, nonsteady state and other substrate contributions. Preserved function and a BHB concentration above 2 mmol/L demonstrate neither oxidation rate nor the fraction of ATP supplied by BHB. Thesis: §2.2; Figs 6-7.

4. How would you separate BHB's fuel effect from its signaling effect and from the sodium in its formulation?

Answer direction: First use matched sodium/osmolality/pH/volume controls. Then combine tracer-based fuel measurements with carefully validated disruption of ketolysis or the specific signaling pathway under study, monitoring off-target effects and baseline function. An effect persisting when oxidation is prevented would support a nonoxidative component, but only if delivery and tissue viability remain comparable. Thesis: §§2.2, 4.2 and 4.5.

5. You infer that lactate accumulation proves sufficient acetyl-CoA from ketones. What alternative explanations fit exactly the same observation?

Answer direction: RBC glycolysis, altered myocardial uptake, competition with pyruvate oxidation, redox changes, regional ischemia, cell injury and absent systemic clearance could all change circuit lactate. Measure arterial-venous fluxes, pyruvate/lactate, oxygen delivery and labeled glucose/BHB metabolism; the causal interpretation in §4.3 is a hypothesis. Do not equate systemic lactate concentration with myocardial lactate production. Thesis: §4.3; Fig 7E.

6. What would count as evidence that ketones improve efficiency, rather than simply enabling more ATP production or sustaining a different workload?

Answer direction: Define efficiency explicitly and measure simultaneous external work and oxygen consumption at matched load and rate, with ATP/ADP or phosphocreatine measurements if claiming energetic benefit. More substrate oxidation is not automatically more work per oxygen. Your MVO2 result alone cannot establish a better P/O ratio, coupling or ATP yield. Thesis: §§1.18 and 3.1; Fig 6G.

7. O-GlcNAc is high in both your high-GI and ketone groups, yet their injury profiles differ. Doesn't that undermine your argument that increased O-GlcNAc is harmful?

Answer direction: It undermines a simple monotonic harmful-marker interpretation. Acute O-GlcNAc elevation can be protective in cardiac ischemia models, while consequences depend on duration, target proteins, cell type and localization; your global blot cannot resolve those distinctions. Frame O-GlcNAc as an associated response requiring mechanistic testing, not proof of injury or misfolded proteins. Thesis: §§3.3 and 4.4.2; Fig 20. Counterpoint primary study: Jones et al 2008.

8. Does a stronger global O-GlcNAc band demonstrate increased hexosamine biosynthesis pathway flux? What else determines the signal?

Answer direction: No: substrate availability, UDP-GlcNAc production, OGT and OGA activity, protein turnover and the identity/abundance of modified proteins all affect the steady-state signal. Measure relevant metabolites, isotope incorporation and OGT/OGA activity or manipulate the pathway with appropriate controls. Use the correct name, hexosamine biosynthesis pathway. Thesis: §§1.15 and 4.4.2; Fig 20.

9. Your thesis treats excess PPP-derived NADPH as a source of ROS, but NADPH also supports antioxidant defense. How would you establish which role dominates?

Answer direction: Quantify oxidative PPP flux and compartment-relevant NADPH/NADP+, glutathione redox state, NOX activity and mitochondrial oxidant production. Test whether selective NOX manipulation changes injury without simply disabling reductive defense. NADPH availability alone does not prove NOX activation or net oxidative harm. Thesis: §1.15.

10. What does your AMPK dataset actually show, and why is deleting the in vivo group not a license to state that both low-GI conditions activate AMPK?

Answer direction: The full comparison was nonsignificant; the reported restricted comparison showed ketones greater than high GI. Unless the comparison was prespecified and justified, interpret it as exploratory and explain multiplicity and variance changes. The thesis does not report significant AMPK activation in both low-GI groups, nor does it measure AMP/ATP to establish energetic stress. Thesis: §3.3; Fig 19; §4.5.

11. Why can AKT Thr308 and Ser473 give different patterns, and what can you infer about insulin signaling from one phosphorylation ratio?

Answer direction: These sites have different upstream control—classically PDK1 for Thr308 and mTORC2 for Ser473—and their occupancy, timing and downstream substrate effects need not match. A lower pThr308/total AKT ratio supports altered signaling but does not directly measure glucose uptake, AKT catalytic output or insulin concentration in tissue. Verify the restricted-group Ser473 analysis and measure relevant downstream targets and transport if those claims matter. Thesis: §3.3; Figs 17-18.

12. Show me the evidence for increased mitophagic flux. How could exactly the same static markers arise when degradation is blocked?

Answer direction: No autophagic or mitophagic flux was directly measured. A validated flux approach needs a temporal readout and degradation controls, such as lysosomal inhibition with LC3/p62 interpreted carefully, together with mitochondrial delivery reporters or equivalent validated measures. AMPK activation, unchanged mTOR phosphorylation, or static mitochondrial protein abundance cannot establish the AMPK-ULK1-Parkin mechanism. Thesis: §4.5; Figs 16-19 and 22.

13. Does a weaker complex I or III immunoblot mean less respiratory capacity, greater electron leak, or fewer mitochondria? How would you distinguish these?

Answer direction: The measured signal could reflect abundance of the antibody-detected subunit, mitochondrial mass, assembly or degradation; it is not a direct enzyme-activity or respiration assay. Normalize to mitochondrial content, examine complex assembly and activity, and measure substrate-specific respiration, coupling and ROS. The significant comparisons reported are high GI versus in vivo, not direct proof of ketone superiority over high GI for ETC function. Thesis: §3.3; Fig 22; §4.5.

14. Your proposed sequence is high GI to ROS to endothelial injury to edema. Where do your results establish the direction of causality?

Answer direction: They establish group associations at the sampled times, not the full sequence; weight gain, injury markers and inflammation could share another driver. Add serial mechanistic measurements, endothelial permeability and junction assays, and targeted interventions under matched osmotic and hemodynamic conditions. Lower cardiac work in low GI could itself reduce some damage markers. Thesis: §§4.4-4.4.1; Figs 8-12.

15. You call ER stress a source of ROS, yet most ER markers are nonsignificant and PDI changes only versus in vivo. Is that enough to establish ER stress, and is O-GlcNAc a measure of ER protein misfolding?

Answer direction: No. Distinguish PDI abundance from activity; identify ATF6 cleavage/activation, IRE1 downstream XBP1 splicing, PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 signaling and functional folding/oxidation measures as possible follow-up assays. Cytosolic/nuclear O-GlcNAcylation is distinct from ER N-linked glycosylation, and global O-GlcNAc abundance is not a direct unfolded-protein assay. Thesis: §§3.3 and 4.4.2; Figs 13-16 and 20.

16. Which compartment's NAD+/NADH ratio is supposed to activate SIRT1 or SIRT3, and why would whole-tissue NAD measurements be insufficient?

Answer direction: Nuclear/cytosolic and mitochondrial redox pools are compartmentalized, while SIRT1 and SIRT3 have different local substrates and regulation. BHB-to-acetoacetate oxidation reduces mitochondrial NAD+, so a general statement that ketones raise the ratio needs actual evidence in this preparation. Measure compartment-relevant redox state, sirtuin activity and target acetylation; no sirtuin activation was measured in the thesis. Thesis: §§1.17 and 4.5.

17. Could the low-GI group look biochemically healthier because it is doing less work and receiving less fuel, despite being a worse transplant?

Answer direction: Yes: reduced activity can reduce some oxidative injury readouts without demonstrating a viable, energetically adequate graft. Compare at matched work where feasible and measure ATP/ADP, phosphocreatine, viability and recovery after physiological substrate restoration. The functional-biochemical dissociation is a central result, not an inconvenience to explain away. Thesis: §§3.1, 3.3 and 4.1; Figs 6 and 8-12.

18. How do you know that differences from in vivo tissue reflect ESHP rather than tissue collection, freezing, blot batch or a change in cell composition?

Answer direction: Describe the actual source and number of independent in vivo hearts, ischemia-to-freezing interval, sampling region, processing and batch balance; clarify whether the samples are paired. LV tissue homogenates include cardiomyocytes, vascular and other cells, so they should not automatically be called isolated myocytes. Validate total-protein normalization, antibody specificity, signal linearity and phospho/total ratios across gels. Thesis: §§2.3 and 3.3.

19. Could changing myocardial acetyl-CoA metabolism alter gene regulation independently of ATP production? How would you test that without assuming mitochondrial acetyl-CoA freely becomes nuclear acetyl-CoA?

Answer direction: Compartmentalized acetyl-CoA provision and protein/histone acetylation are plausible additional mechanisms, but none was measured here. Use validated subcellular fractionation, isotopic tracing, enzyme localization and histone/protein acetylation assays, with contamination controls and attention to timescale. Present this as an extension inspired by metabolic-compartment work, not an explanation already demonstrated by your BHB results. Thesis: §4.5. Background primary study: Sutendra et al 2014.

20. Give me a falsifiable central mechanism and the smallest experiment that could refute it. What outcome would make you stop claiming that BHB rescued a glycolytic bottleneck?

Answer direction: One testable hypothesis is that myocardial BHB oxidation supplies useful oxidative fuel when low GI limits available carbohydrate-derived energy. In matched-vehicle, controlled-load hearts, demonstrate labeled BHB oxidation and its relation to energetics and function, then reduce ketolytic capacity with validated controls. Rescue despite absent BHB oxidation, or rescue reproduced by the sodium/osmotic vehicle, would refute that mechanism as the necessary explanation, even if the intervention remained useful. Thesis: §§1.18, 4.1-4.5.

Three issues to rehearse first for Sutendra

  • Exact ketolysis and redox chemistry, including correction of beta-oxidation and unmeasured NAD+/sirtuin claims.
  • Flux versus concentration or abundance: BHB, lactate, PPP, HBP, O-GlcNAc, ETC and mitophagy.
  • The O-GlcNAc counterexample, exploratory AMPK analyses, and experiments that distinguish mechanism from an associated biomarker.

Twenty difficult questions with answer directions

1. In what physiological sense is glucose below 2 mmol/L with continuous insulin a “fasting state”?

Answer direction: It is an experimental low-glucose, low-insulin condition relative to the comparator, rather than a complete model of whole-body fasting. The circuit lacks normal liver glucose output, adipose lipolysis, endocrine feedback, renal clearance, and autonomic regulation, and it still contains donor blood plus pyruvate and acetate in the modified buffer. Thesis anchor: sections 1.16 and 2.2, Table 2.

2. You reduced both glucose and insulin. Which one caused less edema, lower AKT signaling, and poorer function?

Answer direction: This design cannot separate their independent effects or interaction. A glucose-by-insulin factorial study, with BHB added in a prespecified extension, is needed; mechanistic attribution to either intervention alone exceeds the present comparison. Thesis anchor: Figure 5 and Figure 17.

3. At T7, potassium was 6.57 mmol/L in low GI. Could electrolyte dysfunction explain the functional loss without invoking insufficient myocardial ATP?

Answer direction: It is a plausible contributor, not a proven alternative or mediator. Explain altered membrane potential and excitability, then propose potassium-matched experiments with ATP/phosphocreatine, electrophysiology, and cellular injury measurements; section 4.1's “reduced potassium” wording contradicts Figure 7C. Thesis anchor: Figures 6 and 7C, section 4.1.

4. Explain why raising extracellular potassium first depolarizes a cell yet can eventually impair conduction and contraction.

Answer direction: The potassium equilibrium potential becomes less negative; persistent depolarization can reduce available fast sodium channels and alter action-potential propagation. The exact response depends on concentration, tissue, rate, pH, and other ions; do not turn a reservoir potassium measurement into proof of arrhythmogenesis. Thesis anchor: Figure 7C and section 4.1.

5. Why can an insulin infusion improve extracellular potassium without proving improved mitochondrial energy production?

Answer direction: Insulin stimulates cellular potassium uptake, including through Na+/K+-ATPase regulation, while potassium balance also reflects hemolysis, leakage, and the absence of kidneys. Demonstrate ATP status directly if claiming energetic rescue. Thesis anchor: sections 2.2 and 4.1.

6. Could your lower cardiac index simply reflect a different preload or afterload rather than reduced intrinsic contractility?

Answer direction: Output, stroke work, and dP/dt are load dependent; a stated LA pressure does not fully characterize all loading conditions. Show achieved pressures, volumes, heart rate, coronary pressure, and load-response curves, and consider pressure–volume measures of contractility. Thesis anchor: section 2.2 and Figure 6.

7. Your “cardiac index” divides flow by heart weight. How does this differ from clinical cardiac index, and which heart weight was used?

Answer direction: Clinical cardiac index is normalized to body surface area, whereas this study uses a mass-normalized experimental output. Figure 4 specifies pre-ESHP heart weight, so edema does not directly inflate that denominator; percentage-baseline normalization can still hide absolute differences in starting output. Thesis anchor: Figure 4 and section 3.1.

8. What physiological interpretation can you defend for a percentage of a negative dP/dt minimum?

Answer direction: State whether signed values or magnitudes were normalized and how faster relaxation maps to the plotted ratio. Resolve section 3.1's contradictory statements about whether dP/dt minimum differs; the caption describes a T7 high-GI versus low-GI difference. Thesis anchor: Figure 6D.

9. If myocardial oxygen consumption falls, have you demonstrated metabolic efficiency or simply reduced work?

Answer direction: Lower oxygen consumption alone supports neither interpretation. Compare external work with oxygen consumption under matched loading and include ATP turnover or high-energy phosphates; the study reports MVO2 differences at T3/T5 but no significant group difference at T7. Thesis anchor: Figure 6G.

10. What normal kidney functions are absent from your closed circuit, and how do those absences affect sodium, potassium, acid–base status, and lactate?

Answer direction: Normal excretion, reabsorption, water balance, and acid–base regulation are absent; concentrations therefore reflect circuit inputs, tissue exchange, sampling, and any circuit correction. This makes whole-body clinical thresholds and clearance assumptions incomplete descriptions of the ESHP environment. Thesis anchor: Figure 7 and section 4.2.

11. Why is a human ventricular in silico electrophysiology model insufficient to declare sodium 169 mmol/L safe for the donor heart?

Answer direction: It does not assess the entire organ, coronary endothelium, edema, RBCs, osmotic shifts, implantation, or recipient effects. State that short-term systolic preservation was observed despite sodium accumulation, while safety and longer exposure remain untested. Thesis anchor: Figure 7D and section 4.2.

12. Explain how insulin, glucose availability, BHB oxidation, lactate/pyruvate, and cytosolic versus mitochondrial redox state interact. Which did you measure?

Answer direction: Separate compartment-specific redox couples and substrate fluxes from reservoir concentrations; BHB → acetoacetate generates NADH through BDH1. The study did not measure NAD+/NADH, lactate/pyruvate flux, or BHB oxidation, so the proposed redox explanation remains a hypothesis. Thesis anchor: Figure 2 and sections 1.17, 4.3, and 4.5.

13. Why is preserved coronary or pulmonary-artery outflow not sufficient evidence of intact coronary microvascular function?

Answer direction: Bulk flow cannot establish capillary recruitment, distribution, oxygen extraction, endothelial integrity, or resistance without a pressure gradient. Also explain precisely what PA flow represents in this apparatus rather than treating it as a direct measure of RV contractile performance. Thesis anchor: Figure 3 and Figure 6E.

14. How could lower edema fail to improve your relaxation measurement?

Answer direction: Edema, calcium handling, myocardial energetics, loading, heart rate, and measurement sensitivity all influence relaxation. A small study without a detected ketone–high-GI dP/dt-minimum difference cannot establish that edema has no diastolic consequence. Thesis anchor: Figures 6D and 8, section 4.4.

15. You link TNF-alpha to endothelial leak, yet total NF-kappaB abundance did not differ. Is the inflammatory mechanism contradicted?

Answer direction: Total protein abundance does not measure nuclear translocation, DNA binding, phosphorylation, or cell-specific signaling. The findings establish selected molecular associations, not a completed TNF-alpha–NF-kappaB causal pathway; timing and nonmyocyte contributions remain relevant. Thesis anchor: Figures 12 and 13A.

16. How would restoring systemic or autonomic regulation change the response to BHB after transplantation?

Answer direction: Discuss recipient substrate supply, insulin/glucagon and catecholamines, renal/liver clearance, vascular loading, immune responses, and the denervated graft's altered neural control. Do not claim that an isolated heart with fixed dobutamine reproduces those interactions. Thesis anchor: sections 2.2 and 4.6.1; her renal sympathetic recording study provides a relevant background link.

17. If a graft retains good LV function but has RV dysfunction, how could that worsen recipient renal function?

Answer direction: Raised systemic venous pressure can transmit to the kidney and impair renal perfusion/filtration; reduced forward output can add another burden. Your LV-only biochemical analysis cannot establish RV protection, and whole-recipient outcomes require direct testing. Thesis anchor: section 4.7. Her experimental work specifically examined renal consequences of venous pressure in cardiac/renal dysfunction: Hamza et al. 2022.

18. Why should a healthy juvenile female pig respond like an older DCD donor with diabetes or ventricular dysfunction?

Answer direction: It may not; age, sex, ischemic exposure, insulin sensitivity, coronary disease, and metabolic flexibility can modify the intervention response. State what the controlled model answers and propose staged validation in DCD and comorbid models. Thesis anchor: sections 2.1 and 4.7.

19. Your future-directions section says the liver relies heavily on ketones during fasting. Is that physiologically correct?

Answer direction: Correct the statement: hepatocytes principally produce/export ketones and have extremely low SCOT/OXCT1, preventing substantial terminal ketone oxidation; fasting hepatic energy is supported largely by fatty acid oxidation. Nonparenchymal liver cells are a separate issue, and benefit cannot simply be transferred from heart to liver. Thesis anchor: section 4.6.2. Cotter et al. primary metabolic study.

20. What single result would make you abandon the claim that the ketone protocol improves preservation for recipients?

Answer direction: A well-controlled transplant experiment showing worse graft function, clinically relevant arrhythmias, hemodynamic failure, or recipient injury despite favorable ex situ markers would challenge that claim. Prespecify graft/recipient outcomes and distinguish failure of this formulation from failure of BHB biology in general. Thesis anchor: sections 4.6.1 and 4.8.

Three vulnerabilities to prepare especially well

  • The low-GI condition is not a complete physiological fast, and its two manipulated variables cannot be assigned separate causal effects.
  • Hyperkalemia, hypernatremia, absent organ clearance, loading conditions, and pharmacological support provide alternatives to a purely metabolic explanation.
  • The leap from LV ex situ findings to RV performance, recipient cardiorenal physiology, DCD donors, and transplantation has not yet been tested.
